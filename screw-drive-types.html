Screw Drive Types

Identify screw drive recesses, find the bit size for your screw, and see how Phillips, Pozidriv, Torx, slotted, square and hex drives compare.

What is a screw drive recess?

A drive recess is the shape in the screw head that engages the driver bit. Phillips and Pozidriv have distinct cross-shaped recesses. Torx has six lobes, and an internal hex socket accepts a hex key. An external hex head is turned with a socket or spanner around the head.

Screw drive types at a glance

Head shape and drive type are two separate choices. The head decides how the screw seats against the material; the drive decides how you turn it. This page covers drives only. For head profiles such as pan, countersunk and bugle, see the screw head types guide.

Phillips vs Pozidriv: how to tell them apart

This is the most common mix-up in fastening, and it costs real money in stripped heads. Both are cross recesses, but they are not interchangeable.

Phillips (PH) was designed in the 1930s with tapered flanks that deliberately cam out once a target torque is reached. On hand-built assembly lines that acted as a crude torque limiter and protected the joint. Driven hard with a modern power tool, that same taper is a liability: the bit lifts, chews the recess and rounds it out.

Pozidriv (PZ) keeps the cross but adds four shallow radial ribs set at 45 degrees between the main slots. Those ribs give the bit a near-parallel bearing surface, so it stays seated and transfers noticeably more torque before slipping.

The visual tell: look at the screw head. If you can see four fine tick marks radiating between the arms of the cross, it is Pozidriv. A plain cross with nothing between the arms is Phillips. Bits are stamped PH or PZ followed by the size.

Using a PH bit in a PZ screw, or the reverse, contacts only a fraction of the intended surface and is the fastest way to destroy both. If your line runs Pozidriv screws, make sure the bit stock matches.

Torx and why it dominates powered assembly

Torx uses a six-point star with flanks close to vertical. Because there is almost no wedge angle, virtually none of your driving force is converted into the axial force that pushes a cross-head bit out of the recess. In practice that means higher torque, far less bit wear, less operator fatigue and fewer rejected parts from stripped heads.

For any assembly running impact drivers or automated screwdriving, Torx is usually the right specification. It also allows a smaller recess for the same torque, which lets designers use lower-profile heads.

What is drive size on a screw?

Drive size is the size of the bit that fits the screw head. For example, PH2 means Phillips size 2, PZ2 means Pozidriv size 2, and T20 means Torx size 20. Match both the drive pattern and size to the part drawing or check the bit seats fully before driving. See ISO 8764-1 for cross-recess driver tips.

Slotted, square and hex

  • Slotted is the oldest drive and the weakest. The bit is not self-centring and slides out of the slot sideways under load, so it suits low-torque, hand-driven and restoration work where appearance matters.
  • Square (Robertson) grips the bit well enough that a screw will hang on it unsupported, which makes overhead and one-handed work much faster. It is common in North American joinery and cabinetry.
  • Internal hex (Allen) puts a deep six-sided socket in the head. It transfers high torque in a compact, clean-looking head and is standard on socket cap screws to ISO 4762 and DIN 912.
  • External hex moves the drive to the outside of the head for spanner or socket driving. There is no recess to strip, so it handles the highest torque of all.

Choosing a drive for your assembly

  • Powered, high volume: Torx first, Pozidriv second. Both cut cam-out and bit replacement cost.
  • Hand assembly, torque limiting wanted: Phillips, where controlled cam-out protects a delicate joint.
  • Overhead or one-handed fitting: square drive, for its self-holding grip.
  • Maximum torque in a small head: internal hex.
  • Appearance-led or heritage work: slotted.

FAQ

Screw Drive Types FAQ

What is the difference between Phillips and Pozidriv?
Phillips has four simple tapered slots and is designed to cam out under load. Pozidriv adds four shallow radial ribs at 45 degrees between the main slots, which grip the bit and greatly reduce cam-out. Look for small tick marks between the arms of the cross to identify Pozidriv. Bits are marked PH and PZ and should not be mixed.
Which screw drive gives the highest torque?
Torx (ISO 10664). Its near-vertical flanks generate almost no camming force, so the bit stays seated and both bit and recess last longer. That makes it the usual choice for high-volume powered assembly.
What drive types does C S Fasteners manufacture?
Phillips, Pozidriv, Torx, square (Robertson), internal hex (Allen) and slotted, across our self tapping, machine, wood and chipboard screw lines. Non-standard and security drives can be made to your drawing on our custom line.
What size Phillips bit do I need for a #8 screw?
A #8 screw (about 4.2 mm) normally takes a PH2 or PZ2 bit. PH2 covers roughly #6 to #10, which is why it is the most widely used size. Match the bit to the recess rather than the diameter alone.
